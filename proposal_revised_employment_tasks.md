# Managing Australia's Generative AI Labour Transition

**To:** Deputy Secretary, Employment and Workforce Group, Department of Employment and Workplace Relations  
**From:** Policy Analyst  
**Date:** 11 May 2026  
**Subject:** Policy package for managing generative AI's labour-market impacts

## Executive Summary

Generative artificial intelligence is not simply a job-destroying technology. It is a task-changing general-purpose technology that can raise productivity while redistributing income, bargaining power and opportunity. The central policy problem is not "will AI take all jobs?", but who gains, who bears adjustment costs, where exposed workers are concentrated, and how quickly institutions can adapt. This proposal recommends targeted AI transition accounts, workplace AI impact obligations, and an SME AI adoption and skills diffusion program. The package supports augmentation, reduces displacement, and gives government measurable information about where intervention is needed.

## Problem Definition and Context

AI is changing work through tasks before it changes whole occupations. Jobs combine information search, writing, customer interaction, judgement, supervision, administration and delivery. Generative AI is strongest where tasks are digital, language-based, pattern-recognition heavy and repeatable. Exposure is therefore high in clerical, professional, administrative, software, marketing, finance and customer-service work, but exposure does not automatically mean unemployment.

The critical question is **what kind of technological change is occurring**. Acemoglu and Restrepo argue that automation creates a displacement effect when capital replaces labour in existing tasks, while new tasks create a reinstatement effect when labour gains new productive roles (Acemoglu and Restrepo 2019). If firms use AI mainly to cut labour costs, wages and labour's income share may fall even while output rises. If AI complements workers and diffuses knowledge, productivity gains can be shared.

Australia's background matters. The Productivity Commission argues Australia's near-term AI opportunity is less about building frontier models than adopting AI in local firms and public services (Productivity Commission 2024). Jobs and Skills Australia finds generative AI is more likely to augment work than replace it, but adoption is uneven (Jobs and Skills Australia 2025). The policy issue is an adjustment failure: markets may underinvest in retraining, firms may hide workplace risks, and displaced workers may carry costs not priced into adoption decisions.

## Analysis of Impacts

**Employment.** AI affects job demand through two opposing forces: displacement, where firms need fewer workers for existing work, and reinstatement, where productivity growth creates new roles (Acemoglu and Restrepo 2019). Australian evidence complicates the job-loss story: CSIRO found AI-adopting firms advertised more jobs than comparable non-adopters, although the study mostly covers pre-generative-AI adoption (Radcliffe 2026). The key employment risk is therefore uneven transition: routine clerical and junior professional roles may face slower hiring even if aggregate employment does not collapse.

**Tasks and skills.** Even when jobs remain, their content changes. AI can draft, summarise, classify, translate, search and produce code, so workers shift toward prompting, verification, domain judgement, client communication and accountability. The OECD finds many exposed workers will not need specialist machine-learning skills, but will need changed mixes of digital, cognitive, managerial and social skills (Green 2024). Broad AI literacy and occupation-specific redesign matter more than narrow coding courses.

**Wages and distribution.** Wage effects will be uneven. Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond find a generative AI assistant raised customer-support productivity by 14 per cent on average, with larger gains for less experienced workers (Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond 2023). This suggests AI can compress skill gaps inside firms. But if AI substitutes for routine tasks across the labour market, exposed workers may lose bargaining power. The IMF warns that AI can increase inequality if gains accrue mainly to capital owners and highly complementary workers (Cazzaniga et al. 2024).

**Industry structure.** AI adoption may favour large firms with data, capital and management capacity. Without diffusion support, SMEs may lag and productivity gains may concentrate in large firms. Workers in less digitised firms and regions may face disruption without access to tools that generate new opportunities.

## Policy Options

| Option | Effectiveness | Equity | Feasibility | Main Trade-off |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| AI transition accounts for exposed workers | High if targeted to occupations and regions | Strong, because support follows workers | Medium | Requires good exposure data and training quality control |
| Workplace AI impact obligations | Medium-high for reducing harmful deployment | Strong for transparency and voice | Medium | Compliance costs for employers |
| SME AI adoption and skills diffusion grants | High for productivity diffusion | Medium-high if targeted outside large firms | High | Risk of subsidising adoption that would happen anyway |
| Universal basic income | Low for task transition | Broad but poorly targeted | Low-medium | Expensive and weakly linked to skills transition |

## Recommended Package and Implementation Plan

**First, create AI Transition Accounts** for workers in occupations with high AI exposure, beginning with clerical, administrative, customer-support and junior professional roles. Accounts should fund accredited short courses, career advice, paid training leave and recognition of prior learning. Eligibility should be based on occupation, industry, region and displacement risk, because the goal is prevention.

**Second, require Workplace AI Impact Statements** for employers above 100 staff when AI is used for hiring, performance management, rostering, surveillance or major task redesign. Statements should ask what system is used, why it is adopted, which tasks change, who is affected, how workers were consulted, what risks exist, and how outcomes will be reviewed. This reduces information asymmetry and makes firms internalise adjustment costs.

**Third, fund an SME AI Diffusion Program** through industry bodies, TAFEs and chambers. Grants should support adoption projects only where firms co-invest and train workers. Priority should go to low-productivity, high-employment sectors such as business services, retail administration, care administration and logistics.

**Timeline.** In the first six months, DEWR should establish an AI Labour Market Taskforce with Jobs and Skills Australia, unions, employers and state training authorities. Within 12 months, pilot transition accounts in three sectors and publish impact statement templates. Within 24 months, expand nationally and link subsidies to employment and wage outcomes.

**Responsibilities.** DEWR should lead policy design. Jobs and Skills Australia should maintain exposure mapping. State training systems and TAFEs should deliver accredited training. The Fair Work Ombudsman should receive impact statements, while privacy and discrimination regulators handle unlawful uses.

**KPIs.** Success should be measured by training completion, employment retention, wage growth of exposed workers, SME adoption, consultation, and AI-related workplace disputes.

## Risks and Mitigations

The first risk is training waste. Generic AI courses may become low-value credentials. This should be mitigated by funding occupation-specific training tied to employer demand.

The second risk is regulatory overreach. If impact statements become paperwork, firms may resist adoption. Templates should be simple, risk-based and focused on consequential uses.

The third risk is inequitable access. Better educated workers may benefit first. Government should prioritise lower-paid clerical workers, regional workers, women in administrative roles, and SME workers.
