# Economic Analysis of AI and Labour Markets

Generative AI's labour-market effects are likely to first appear in information-intensive white-collar work because LLM-powered software primarily applies to text, code and image-based tasks (Eloundou et al. 2024). This is already visible in Australian workplaces: Jobs and Skills Australia (2025) reports GenAI use cases including improved coding efficiency, preparing first drafts, summarising information, preparing meeting minutes, searching for task-relevant information and preliminary data analysis. This task profile explains why international evidence shows exposure across the office hierarchy: the ILO finds that routine clerical work has the highest overall exposure, while OECD evidence shows that non-routine, high-skill white-collar occupations are also heavily exposed (OECD 2023; Gmyrek et al. 2025).

New technologies, automation and AI change labour demand through displacement and reinstatement effects: they can replace labour in existing tasks while also creating new tasks where humans retain comparative advantage. The employment risk is therefore not simple mass unemployment, but a timing problem: displacement can occur quickly when firms automate existing tasks, while reinstatement depends on slower processes such as new job design, worker retraining and complementary investment (Acemoglu and Restrepo 2019). This transition can be costly because firms may not automatically retrain exposed workers; evidence from automation-exposed workers shows lower participation in firm-financed training, which can leave displaced workers without enough support to move into new tasks (Heß, Janssen and Leber 2023).

The deeper effect is task redesign. Technology has historically substituted for routine cognitive tasks while complementing non-routine problem-solving and communication (Autor, Levy and Murnane 2003). In white-collar work, this means AI can perform first drafts, search and basic analysis, while humans shift toward verification, judgement, client trust and accountability. Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond's peer-reviewed customer-support study shows this can be productive: AI assistance raised productivity by 15 per cent on average and helped less experienced workers most (Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond 2025). However, this also creates a career-pipeline risk: if AI performs the basic tasks juniors once learned from, entry-level skill formation may weaken.

Wage effects are likely uneven. Higher productivity does not guarantee higher worker pay. Automation can reduce labour's share even while output rises, and Australian parliamentary analysis warns AI gains may widen inequality if some occupations and firms benefit more than others (Acemoglu and Restrepo 2019; Parliamentary Library 2025). Industry structure may also shift: large firms with data, capital and AI infrastructure can adopt faster, while SMEs may lag. The policy problem is therefore uneven transition, not simply job loss.
