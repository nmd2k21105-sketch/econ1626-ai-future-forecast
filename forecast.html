Section 01 —

The Central Argument

Not whether AI will change legal services, but who captures the gains.

The Distributional Question

A Different Species of Technology Demands a Different Kind of Analysis

In August 2025, the President of the Australian Law Reform Commission told the Australian Law Forum that AI regulation is "the largest and most complex law reform exercise that currently confronts Australia", and that the public debate had barely recognised it was a law reform exercise at all (Bromberg 2025). This official ALRC intervention frames the legal-policy problem: AI is not just another product liability issue, but a technology that is trained, adaptive, and increasingly capable of directing itself.

This essay makes a narrower economic claim. Using the task-based framework of Acemoglu and Restrepo (2019), the routine-task hypothesis of Autor, Levy and Murnane (2003), and Berg's capital-labour hybrid framing, the disruption AI brings to Australian legal services is a story about who captures the productivity gains when AI automates routine cognitive tasks previously performed by junior lawyers. Current Australian firm-level evidence suggests those gains are already measurable; the theoretical question is where the value flows.

Central Forecast

By 2027–2030, AI will automate a substantial share of junior associate task hours at large Australian commercial law firms. Unless reinstatement through new AI-supervision, governance, and client-facing tasks is strong enough to offset displacement, productivity gains will concentrate among equity partners and AI vendors. The junior career pipeline will narrow, and Australia's fragmented voluntary regulatory framework will struggle to shape how these gains are distributed without structural reform.

Section 03 —

The Economic Mechanism

Why this pattern was predictable, and where the theory says it goes next.

Economic Theory Applied to Australian Law

Displacement, Reinstatement, and the Diffusion Lag

Acemoglu and Restrepo (2019) model technological change through two forces: the displacement effect (capital substitutes for labour in existing tasks, reducing labour's income share) and the reinstatement effect (new tasks are created where labour has comparative advantage, recovering the share). The distributional outcome depends on which force dominates. Autor, Levy and Murnane (2003) identify which tasks are exposed: computers substitute most readily for routine cognitive tasks (information processing, pattern recognition, document analysis) and complement non-routine tasks requiring judgment, persuasion, and contextual reasoning.

Legal services sit at the exact fault line. Document review, legal research, and first-draft preparation are routine cognitive tasks. Novel legal argument, client advocacy, and interpersonal trust are non-routine. Junior lawyers perform mostly the former; senior partners perform mostly the latter. Robb et al. (2025) confirmed this through semi-structured interviews with 51 practitioners across 27 Australian organisations; participants described significant reductions in demand for junior tasks including document review, research, and contract drafting, and warned of an emerging "5-year problem": it is unclear how junior lawyers will develop fundamental skills if Gen AI assumes responsibility for the tasks that provided that training. Martin et al. (2024) confirmed the exposure empirically: LLMs match senior lawyer accuracy on contract review while operating at 99.97% lower cost. Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond (2023) measured the productivity effect across 5,172 workers, recording a 14% average gain with a 34% improvement for novice and low-skilled workers. Crucially, the most experienced workers saw small gains in speed but small declines in quality when relying on AI, a pattern Dell'Acqua et al. (2023) term the jagged frontier: AI improves novice output while slightly degrading expert quality through over-reliance.

Berg (2026) places this in the broader GPT framework: like electricity and computing before it, AI is a general-purpose technology whose full impact requires complementary organisational change, not just tool adoption. Australian firms are currently in the bolt-on phase, deploying AI onto existing workflows rather than redesigning around what AI can actually do (Berg 2026). When that redesign occurs, the displacement effect will intensify. Acemoglu (2024) argues the aggregate TFP impact will be modest (less than 0.55% over ten years) because only a fraction of tasks are currently exposed to profitable automation. But his own model predicts AI will widen the gap between capital and labour income even when it raises worker productivity, because capital owners capture the returns from AI-driven gains.

Section 04 —

Who Captures the Gains?

The productivity is real. The distribution is the problem.

The Distributional Mechanism

From Wages to Profit: The Institutional Reclassification

Only 6% of Australian law firms charge clients less for AI-assisted work; 34% charge more; 58% have not changed rates (Law Society Journal 2025). Productivity gains are being retained by firms. Berg (2026) identifies this as institutional reclassification: returns that previously flowed as wages to junior associates now flow as profit to equity partners and as API fees to AI vendors. Before AI, document review value was split between the associate's wage and the firm's margin. After AI, Harvey produces the document, the firm pays an API fee, and the surplus flows entirely upward. This is why the productivity–wage link breaks: worker wages are determined by the scarcity of their tasks, not by aggregate firm productivity. When AI makes a task abundant, the workers who performed it lose bargaining power even as the firm becomes more productive. Acemoglu (2024) formalises this: AI widens the capital–labour income gap specifically because returns from AI-driven efficiency accrue to capital owners, not to the workers who were replaced.

The privilege dimension compounds the distributional problem. When client data is processed through third-party AI platforms, legal professional privilege may be inadvertently waived, as the data has been disclosed to a non-lawyer third party. United States v Heppner (S.D.N.Y. 2026) established that documents processed through a public AI platform lose privilege; the court found disclosure to a public AI tool constitutes third-party waiver. Queensland's Supreme Court (guideline, 15 September 2025) warns that "any information that you input into a public Generative AI chatbot should be seen as being published to all the world" (Hamilton Locke 2025). Where privilege is waived, companies cannot resist ASIC section 30 production notices.

"Work that previously took a junior four hours is now being completed in under one hour via the latest AI. The work hasn't gone away. It's moved up the chain."

Section 05 —

Three Scenarios to 2030

The trajectory is not predetermined. Institutional response is the critical variable.

2025–2030 · Forecast

Baseline, Upside, Downside — Compared Across Six Criteria

Baseline: Concentrated Gains, Narrowing Pipeline (Most Likely)

Scenario logic: AI adoption continues unevenly. Large firms use AI to raise productivity and retain gains, while new AI-governance roles only partly offset junior-task displacement.

  • MinterEllison-style intake cuts continue through 2028.
  • Workplace AI guidance remains uneven across the profession.
  • State-by-state guidance persists without a national statutory framework.
Policy trigger: A high-profile AI legal error (fabricated precedent in a Federal Court submission, or an inadvertent privilege waiver causing ASIC enforcement) forces binding national standards.

Upside: Productivity Shared, Access Expanded

Scenario logic: Competition and policy pressure force firms to share AI productivity gains. New AI-governance, privacy, verification, and client-advisory tasks absorb displaced junior work.

  • Clients demand lower bills for AI-assisted work.
  • Law schools redesign training around verification and AI-complementary judgement.
  • Public support helps small firms and community legal centres access safe AI tools.
What must be true: Competitive pressure is sufficient before concentration entrenches large-firm dominance. Reinstatement (Acemoglu & Restrepo 2019) offsets displacement. Government acts before the access gap becomes irreversible.

Downside: Concentration, Exclusion, Regulatory Failure

Scenario logic: Large firms capture AI gains without passing them to clients or junior workers. Regulation remains reactive, and smaller practices lack the governance capacity to compete safely.

  • AI-generated work is billed at premium rates without genuine supervision.
  • Smaller practices face higher compliance burdens and weaker tool access.
  • A two-tier profession emerges between AI-equipped commercial firms and under-resourced practices.
Trigger: Regulatory inaction past 2027. Major AI legal scandal without legislative response. Market concentration entrenched before standards are set.

Section 06 —

What Australia Must Do

Three concrete gaps, and instruments to close them without entrenching BigLaw.

Policy Recommendations

Liability Over Licensing — Before the Window Closes

Australia's current position, comprising existing laws supplemented by voluntary state guidance (Australian Government 2025), ASIC's "Year of Accountability" (Brief Online 2026), and state court practice notes (ACT Law Society 2024), is coherent but structurally inadequate. Bromberg (2025) identifies the core problem: Australia is debating the form of AI regulation (one Act versus amending existing laws) without first identifying the harms AI creates and whether current law addresses them. Three gaps demand immediate attention.

Gap 1: Liability for AI legal errors. When AI-drafted work causes client harm, liability falls on the supervising lawyer, but no binding standard defines what adequate supervision requires (ACT Law Society 2024). Kretschmer et al. (2023) propose differentiating endogenous harm (developers or deployers could have foreseen and prevented the failure, so strict liability applies) from exogenous harm (genuinely unforeseeable, attracting limited liability). This allocates incentives correctly without over-regulating innovation and is more robust than prescriptive ex-ante licensing that becomes obsolete before it can be enforced.

Gap 2: Legal professional privilege. Third-party AI processing may waive privilege inadvertently. United States v Heppner (S.D.N.Y. 2026) established this in federal court; Queensland's Supreme Court (15 September 2025) has issued the same warning (Hamilton Locke 2025). The joint NSW, Victorian, and WA regulators statement is explicit: practitioners must not input confidential, sensitive, or privileged information into public AI tools (ACT Law Society 2024). Yet no binding national standard currently exists; state-by-state guidance remains unenforceable at the federal level.

Gap 3: Market concentration. Kretschmer et al. (2023) warn that EU-style risk-based regulation tends to entrench incumbents by imposing compliance costs large firms absorb and small firms cannot. Australia must calibrate any regulatory response so it protects small practices and community legal centres rather than creating barriers that accelerate BigLaw dominance.

Immediate 2026 — Law Council + ASIC

  • Binding national AI supervision standards, not voluntary guidance
  • ASIC sector-specific AI governance guidance for legal practices
  • KPI: 90% of top-50 firms with documented AI supervision protocols by Dec 2026

Short Term 2027–28 — ALRC + Federal Court

  • ALRC statutory review: identify liability gaps; enact developer/deployer liability allocation (Kretschmer et al. 2023)
  • Federal Court finalises AI practice note, establishing a national standard for AI-generated submissions
  • KPI: Privilege waiver risk addressed in binding guidelines across all federal jurisdictions

Medium Term 2029–30 — Parliament + Law Schools

  • National AI liability framework for professional services, enacted rather than voluntary
  • Law school accreditation updated to require AI competence and verification training
  • Subsidised AI access program for community legal centres and firms under 10 staff

What to Avoid

  • EU-style ex-ante conformity assessment, which entrenches BigLaw through compliance costs (Kretschmer et al. 2023)
  • Technology-specific legislation that becomes obsolete before enforced
  • Regulatory inaction past 2027: the window to shape distribution is closing

Section 07 —

Conclusion

The gains are real. The question is who keeps them.

2025–2030 · The Stakes

Before the Market Makes the Decision for Us

The disruption AI is bringing to Australian legal services is not a prediction. It is a 2025 fact: MinterEllison has cut 31% of its graduate intake and cited AI as the reason (Lawyers Weekly 2025). Holding Redlich completes research in 30 minutes that once took 4.5 hours (CAIDE 2025). The Law Society Journal data shows junior billable hours falling for the third consecutive half-year while senior hours rise, and only 6% of firms charge clients less for AI-assisted work (Law Society Journal 2025). The productivity gains (58 times faster document review, $1.5 million saved per major matter) are being retained by firms, not passed to clients or shared with workers.

The economic mechanism is exactly what Autor, Levy and Murnane (2003) predicted: routine cognitive tasks automated first. Acemoglu and Restrepo (2019) showed the distributional outcome depends on whether new tasks are created, and Acemoglu (2024) shows that AI specifically widens the capital–labour income gap even in scenarios where it raises worker productivity. Berg (2026) frames this as institutional reclassification: the value that once flowed as wages to junior associates now flows as profit to equity partners and API fees to AI vendors.

The question this essay asks is not whether the billing hour will die. It is whether Australia's institutions (the ALRC, Law Council, ASIC, and Parliament) move fast enough to shape the distribution of gains before the market resolves it entirely in favour of those who are already winning. Bromberg (2025) warned that without foundational law reform, "the law will be left to chase a tail it will never ever catch." The tail is moving fast.

"Whether Artificial Intelligence is regulated and whatever form that regulation takes, AI is and will remain an agent of change that will transform the legal profession. And so it should."

— Justice Mordy Bromberg, ALRC President, Australian Law Forum, 14 August 2025
