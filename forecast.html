The Central Argument

The economic question is not whether AI will change legal services. It is who captures the gains.

August 2025 · Canberra

The Largest Law Reform Exercise Australia Has Ever Faced

In August 2025, the President of the Australian Law Reform Commission stood before the inaugural Australian Law Forum and delivered a diagnosis that most of his audience was not ready to hear. AI regulation, said Justice Mordy Bromberg, is "the largest and most complex law reform exercise that currently confronts Australia" — and the public debate has barely recognised it is a law reform exercise at all (Bromberg 2025).

Bromberg's point was not that artificial intelligence will destroy the legal profession. He was careful to say the opposite — that forecasts of mass unemployment among lawyers are "likely misplaced," that AI cannot replicate the wisdom, the interpersonal trust, or the contextual judgment that makes a good lawyer valuable. What he was saying is more precise and more troubling: that the law was designed for a world without AI intermediaries, and that Australia is making regulatory decisions before it has done the foundational work of identifying what harms AI creates and whether current law can address them.

This essay makes a more specific argument. The disruption AI is bringing to Australian legal services is not primarily a story about robots replacing lawyers. It is a story about who captures the productivity gains when artificial intelligence automates the cognitive tasks that junior lawyers have always performed — and who bears the cost when those tasks disappear from the entry-level career pipeline.

"If we do not do that, the law will be left to chase a tail it will never ever catch."

— Justice Mordy Bromberg, ALRC President, Australian Law Forum, 14 August 2025

The distributional question is the economic question. And Australia has not begun to answer it.

Central Forecast

By 2027–2030, AI will automate a substantial share of junior associate task hours in Australian commercial law firms. Productivity gains will concentrate among equity partners and large firms. The junior career pipeline will narrow. Australia's fragmented voluntary regulatory framework — no national AI statute, unresolved liability, state-by-state guidance — is structurally inadequate to shape how these gains are distributed.

2024–2025: The Quiet Revolution

Firm by firm, the work moves up the chain.

2023–2025 · Australian Law Firms

While the Debate Continues, the Profession Reorganises

The adoption did not happen in a single announcement. It happened through a sequence of quiet decisions made by managing partners across Sydney and Melbourne, each looking at a tool that could do in minutes what a graduate had always done in hours.

In August 2023, Allens became the first major Australian firm to deploy a firm-wide generative AI platform — "Airlie," built on Microsoft Azure — to "automate routine tasks, support drafting efforts, and improve efficiency" (CAIDE 2025). By February 2025, Gilbert + Tobin had launched Harvey AI across its 650-lawyer team. Ashurst had signed a global partnership making it the first firm to deploy Harvey across every practice group. MinterEllison had deployed Lantern for discovery. Holding Redlich had permanently adopted Lexis+ AI. Clayton Utz was using AI to generate first drafts of client advice, internal emails, and court filings (CAIDE 2025).

Each announcement was carefully worded. Nobody mentioned headcount. The language was always efficiency, always productivity. Then came the signal that changed the conversation.

In 2025, MinterEllison — one of Australia's top four law firms — announced it was reducing its graduate intake from 104 to 72 positions. A 31% cut. Its chief people officer Rachel Banks stated directly that "responsible use of AI is improving efficiency in routine work graduates traditionally start on, while demand shifts towards complex matters" (Lawyers Weekly 2025). For the first time, a major Australian law firm had publicly connected AI adoption to reduced entry-level hiring.

The billable hours data confirmed a structural shift. Non-equity partners and junior to mid-level associates recorded their third consecutive productivity decline in the first half of 2025/26. Senior associates and equity partners logged more hours. The profession's internal labour market was reorganising — value flowing upward, volume being absorbed by machines. While 86% of large firms plan to grow overall associate ranks through 2027, only 35% plan to boost first-year lawyer classes and only 37% plan to increase summer associate hiring (Law Society Journal 2025). The profession is growing at the top while contracting at the entry point.

The Adoption Gap

74% of large Australian law firms (200+ employees) have formal AI policies. Only 25% of small firms do — and 11% of small firm professionals admit to using unapproved AI tools independently (Agile Market Intelligence 2026). This is not only a technology adoption story. It is an early signal of market concentration: the firms with resources to move first are building capability advantages that regional practices and community legal centres cannot yet match.

The Economic Mechanism

Why this pattern was predictable — and what the theory says about where it goes next.

Economic Theory Applied to Law

Displacement, Reinstatement, and the Diffusion Lag

The economic framework for understanding what is happening in Australian law firms was built long before ChatGPT existed. Acemoglu and Restrepo (2019) model technological change as operating through two opposing forces. The displacement effect: capital substitutes for labour in tasks labour previously performed, reducing the labour share of value added. The reinstatement effect: new tasks are created in which labour has a comparative advantage, recovering the labour share. The net outcome depends on which force dominates, and on the size of the productivity gain from automation.

Autor, Levy and Murnane (2003) provide the task taxonomy that makes this operational. Computers — and now AI — substitute most readily for routine cognitive tasks: information processing, pattern recognition, rule-following, document analysis. They complement non-routine cognitive tasks: complex judgment, novel problem-solving, persuasion, and contextual reasoning. Legal services sit at a precise fault line between these categories. Document review is routine. Novel legal argument is not. Junior lawyers do mostly the former. Senior partners do mostly the latter.

Martin et al. (2024) tested this directly — comparing large language models against junior lawyers and legal process outsourcers on real contract review tasks. LLMs matched or exceeded junior lawyer accuracy, completed reviews in seconds rather than hours, and operated at a 99.97% cost reduction. The tasks most exposed to AI substitution are precisely the entry-level tasks through which junior lawyers build their professional experience and earn their early career wage premium.

Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond (2023) measured the productivity effect across 5,172 customer support agents. Average productivity rose 15%. Crucially, the distribution mattered: less experienced workers improved both speed and quality substantially, while the most experienced and highest-skilled workers saw small gains in speed but small declines in quality. This is the jagged frontier: AI genuinely helps novices while slightly degrading the output quality of experts who over-rely on it. Applied to legal services, AI closes the gap between junior and senior performance in the short run — while creating a long-run risk that junior lawyers who rely on AI never develop the independent judgment that makes them valuable partners in the future.

The Diffusion Lag — Why Adoption Is Uneven

Berg (2026) draws on the historical pattern of general-purpose technologies: steam, electricity, computing all required decades of complementary innovation before their full productivity impact appeared in aggregate data. The electricity lesson is directly applicable to legal AI. Early factories simply replaced steam engines with electric motors while keeping the same layout and workflows — and saw disappointing productivity gains. Full productivity only came when factories were redesigned around electricity's flexibility: new floor plans, new worker roles, new management structures.

Australian law firms are in the bolt-on phase. MinterEllison saves $1.5 million per discovery matter. Holding Redlich completes research tasks nine times faster. But the underlying workflow — partners reviewing associate work, associates developing through document review — has not yet been redesigned around what AI can actually do. When that redesign occurs, the productivity impact will be far larger. And so will the displacement effect on junior positions.

One important caveat: Acemoglu (2024) argues in a peer-reviewed paper that the macroeconomic productivity gains from AI are likely far smaller than popular accounts suggest — estimating total factor productivity gains of no more than 0.55% over the next decade. AI's impact is concentrated in easy-to-learn tasks; the harder, context-dependent tasks that dominate senior legal work are far less exposed. This essay does not dispute the aggregate modesty of the macroeconomic impact. The argument operates at the sector and task level: even if AI adds little to national GDP, it can substantially restructure who performs which tasks within the legal profession — and Acemoglu (2024) himself finds that AI "is predicted to widen the gap between capital and labor income" even when it raises low-skill worker productivity.

Who Captures the Gains?

The productivity is real. The question is whether it flows to partners, clients, or workers.

The Distributional Question

From Wages to Profit: The Institutional Reclassification

The billing data answers the distributional question more clearly than the employment data. Only 6% of Australian law firms charge clients less for AI-assisted work. A further 34% charge more. The remaining 58% have not changed their rates at all (Law Society Journal 2025). The productivity gains created by AI — tools that compress hours of junior work into minutes — are being retained by firms rather than passed to clients or shared with workers.

Berg (2026) identifies the underlying mechanism as an institutional reclassification: returns that previously flowed as wages to workers now flow as profit to capital owners and to AI vendors. Before AI, a law firm hired a junior associate, paid wages, and the value of the associate's document review was split between the associate (wage) and the firm (profit margin). After AI, the firm subscribes to Harvey, pays API fees, and the value of the same document review flows entirely to the firm and to Anthropic or OpenAI as the vendor. The junior associate is not necessarily fired — but the number of such positions required shrinks, and the tasks that remain increasingly involve supervising AI output rather than producing legal work independently.

Acemoglu (2024) provides the theoretical underpinning for why this distribution is not an accident. Even when AI improves the productivity of low-skill workers in certain tasks, this can increase rather than reduce inequality — because the capital owners who deploy AI capture the returns, not the workers who use it. AI is specifically predicted to widen the gap between capital and labour income, even in scenarios where it raises worker productivity. Applied to legal services: the firm's equity partners (capital owners) benefit from AI-driven efficiency gains, while the junior lawyers (labour) face a narrowing entry-level market.

"Work that previously took a junior four hours is now being completed in under one hour via the latest AI. In simple terms, the work hasn't gone away. It's moved up the chain."

— Nandan Subramaniam, principal at Zed Law, quoted in Law Society Journal (2025)

There is a competing argument worth taking seriously. The International Bar Association (2024) notes that 52% of in-house legal teams expect bills to decrease as AI adoption spreads — suggesting competitive pressure will eventually force firms to pass gains to clients. If clients capture the value through lower fees, the distributional harm falls primarily on junior lawyers and small firms, not on corporate clients. In either case, the concentration dynamic worsens.

The privilege dimension adds a further distributional complexity that current law has not resolved. When client data is processed through third-party AI platforms, legal professional privilege may be inadvertently waived — because data has been disclosed to a non-lawyer third party. The Heppner case (US, 2026) established this risk in federal court. Queensland's Supreme Court has already warned that "any information input into public chatbots should be seen as published." Where privilege is waived, companies cannot resist ASIC section 30 production notices on privilege grounds — creating regulatory exposure that currently falls without a clear liability pathway (Hamilton Locke 2025).

Three Scenarios to 2030

The trajectory is not predetermined. Institutional response is the critical variable.

2025–2030

Baseline, Upside, and Downside — A Systematic Comparison

The following scenarios share the same starting conditions. They diverge based on three variables: the pace of AI adoption among small firms, the strength of the reinstatement effect through new legal tasks, and the speed of Australia's institutional response. The scenarios are presented narratively below and compared systematically across explicit economic criteria in the table that follows.

Baseline (2025–2030): Concentrated Gains, Narrowing Pipeline

Key assumption: Adoption continues at current pace. Large firms widen advantage. Voluntary regulation persists. New AI governance roles emerge in smaller numbers than displaced positions.

  • Graduate intakes at top-tier firms shrink 5–10% annually through 2028 as AI handles document review and research
  • Mid-tier and small firms adopt AI more slowly — 74% vs 25% policy gap becomes a sustained capability gap
  • Equity partner billing rates rise as judgment-intensive work becomes relatively scarcer and more valuable
  • New roles emerge — AI output reviewers, legal data scientists — but in smaller numbers than displaced junior positions
  • Australia issues updated voluntary guidance but no national AI statute — fragmented state-by-state framework persists
  • The access-to-justice gap widens: community legal centres and small practices lack the resources to match frontier firm capability
Policy trigger: A high-profile AI legal error — fabricated precedent used in a Federal Court submission, or an inadvertent privilege waiver causing regulatory exposure — accelerates the ALRC's law reform exercise and forces binding national standards.

Upside (2025–2030): Productivity Shared, Access Expanded

Key assumption: Competition forces firms to pass AI gains to clients. New legal tasks absorb displaced workers. Institutions adapt faster than the baseline assumes.

  • Competitive pressure from AI-empowered clients forces firms to reduce hourly rates — the 52% of in-house teams expecting lower bills get them (IBA 2024)
  • AI-powered community legal centre tools (amica.gov.au, SmartAssist, NALA) expand access to justice for underserved Australians
  • Law schools redesign curricula around AI-complementary skills — critical reasoning, client management, AI output verification
  • New legal specialisms in AI governance, data privacy, and algorithmic accountability create genuine reinstatement
  • Australia adopts a national AI liability framework allocating responsibility clearly between developers, deployers, and practitioners
What has to be true: Competitive pressure is sufficient to force price reductions before market concentration entrenches large-firm dominance. The reinstatement effect (Acemoglu & Restrepo 2019) operates strongly enough to offset displacement. Government acts before the access-to-justice gap becomes irreversible.

Downside (2025–2030): Concentration, Exclusion, Regulatory Failure

Key assumption: Large firms capture gains without passing them on. Regulatory vacuum persists. AI errors cause systemic harm. The profession stratifies into two tiers.

  • The 74% vs 25% AI adoption gap becomes permanent — large firms pull decisively ahead, small firms lose clients to AI-enabled commercial practices
  • Graduate employment in law falls as aggregate intake declines and new roles do not materialise at scale
  • AI legal errors — hallucinated citations, inadvertent privilege waivers — generate client harm without clear liability pathways
  • Firms bill at partner rates for AI-generated work never reviewed with genuine care; clients bear the cost of both higher fees and lower quality
  • ASIC's 2026 accountability agenda creates compliance costs that burden small practices disproportionately without adequate guidance
  • Australia develops a two-tier legal profession: frontier AI-equipped commercial firms serving corporate clients; under-resourced practices serving everyone else
What triggers this: Regulatory inaction past 2027. A major AI legal scandal without legislative response. Failure to develop national AI competence standards before market concentration becomes entrenched.

What Australia Must Do

Three concrete gaps — and the regulatory instruments to close them without entrenching BigLaw.

Policy Analysis

Liability Over Licensing — Closing Three Gaps That Cannot Wait

Australia's current position is coherent but insufficient. The December 2025 National AI Plan confirmed that existing laws — the Corporations Act, the Privacy Act, professional conduct rules — will govern AI for now, supplemented by voluntary state-level guidance (Australian Government 2025). ASIC declared 2026 its "Year of Accountability," signalling civil penalties and criminal referrals against directors failing to oversee AI risks (Brief Online 2026). State law societies have issued guidance on confidentiality and hallucination risks (ACT Law Society 2024).

But Bromberg (2025) is correct that the debate has fixated on form — one AI Act versus amending existing laws — rather than substance. The foundational exercise of identifying what harms AI creates in legal services, and whether current law addresses them, has not been done. Three gaps are concrete enough to act on now.

Gap 1: Liability for AI legal errors. When an AI-drafted contract causes client harm, existing law places liability on the supervising lawyer. But this assumes lawyers can meaningfully supervise AI output — a questionable assumption when AI systems hallucinate with apparent confidence. The Law Council's guidance that "the duty of competence extends to supervising AI output as it does to supervising a junior solicitor" is correct in principle but creates an accountability standard that is unenforceable without clearer definitions of what adequate supervision requires (ACT Law Society 2024).

Kretschmer et al. (2023) offer the key insight: regulation should differentiate between endogenous harm — where the AI developer or deployer could have foreseen and prevented the failure — and exogenous harm — where the harm arises from unforeseeable edge cases. Making developers and deployers financially liable for endogenous harms signals to the industry that AI accuracy and supervision must be invested in, without creating unworkable obligations for edge cases no reasonable precaution could prevent. This is more robust than prescriptive ex-ante licensing because it does not require regulators to predict in advance exactly what "safe" AI looks like in every context.

Gap 2: Legal professional privilege. When client data is processed through third-party AI platforms, privilege may be inadvertently waived. The Heppner case (US, 2026) established this risk in federal court. Queensland's Supreme Court has already warned that "any information input into public chatbots should be seen as published." Australian courts have not yet ruled definitively, but the risk is real and unmanaged by any binding national standard (Hamilton Locke 2025). The Privacy Act amendments effective December 2026 will require disclosure of substantially automated decision-making affecting individuals — but they do not resolve the privilege question.

Gap 3: Market concentration and access to justice. The 74% versus 25% AI policy gap between large and small firms risks becoming structural. Kretschmer et al. (2023) warn that EU-style risk-based regulation tends to entrench incumbents by imposing compliance costs that large firms absorb easily and small practices cannot. Australia must design any regulatory response with this dynamic explicitly in mind — calibrating standards so they are proportionate to firm size and practice context, not one-size-fits-all obligations that large firms can satisfy with compliance departments and small firms cannot.

Immediate (2026) — Who: Law Council + ASIC

  • Law Council to issue binding national AI competence standards with explicit supervision requirements — not voluntary guidance
  • ASIC to issue sector-specific AI governance guidance for legal practices advising on corporate matters
  • KPI: 90% of top-50 firms with documented AI supervision protocols by Dec 2026

Short Term (2027–2028) — Who: ALRC + Federal Court

  • ALRC to complete statutory review identifying liability gaps — with explicit developer/deployer liability allocation (Kretschmer et al. 2023)
  • Federal Court to finalise AI practice note, creating a national standard for AI-generated submissions
  • KPI: Privilege waiver risk addressed in binding court guidelines across all federal jurisdictions

Medium Term (2029–2030) — Who: Parliament + Law Schools

  • National AI liability framework for high-stakes professional services — enacted, not voluntary
  • Law school accreditation updated to require AI competence and verification training
  • Subsidised AI tool access program for community legal centres and small firms (<10 staff)
  • KPI: Access-to-justice AI tool usage measurably increased in CLCs by 2030

What to Avoid — Risk: Entrenching BigLaw

  • EU-style ex-ante conformity assessment — compliance costs entrench large firms (Kretschmer et al. 2023)
  • Technology-specific legislation that becomes obsolete faster than it can be amended
  • Treating AI as equivalent to existing legal software — it is "a different species of technology" (Bromberg 2025) and its regulation requires a different species of analysis
  • Regulatory inaction past 2027 — the window for shaping distribution is closing

Conclusion

The billing hour is not dead. But the foundations beneath it are being hollowed out.

2025–2030 · The Stakes

The Gains Are Real. The Question Is Who Keeps Them.

The disruption AI is bringing to Australian legal services did not begin with a manifesto. It began in conference rooms where managing partners looked at tools that could do in 30 minutes what a junior had always done in 4.5 hours, and decided that efficiency was the imperative. It has proceeded through a sequence of firm-level decisions — Airlie at Allens, Harvey at Gilbert + Tobin, Lantern at MinterEllison — each individually sensible, collectively transformative.

The economic mechanism is well understood. Autor, Levy and Murnane (2003) predicted that computers would substitute for routine cognitive tasks. Acemoglu and Restrepo (2019) showed that the distributional outcome depends on whether new tasks are created to absorb displaced workers. Brynjolfsson et al. (2023) showed that generative AI raises productivity — most for the least experienced workers — while the jagged frontier finding warns that over-reliance on AI slightly degrades expert quality and may hollow out the apprenticeship model through which the profession has always developed its future partners.

Acemoglu (2024) urges caution about the macroeconomic scale — and his estimate of less than 0.55% TFP growth over the next decade may well be right. But his own model predicts that AI will widen the gap between capital and labour income even as it raises productivity. In legal services, that means equity partners and AI vendors capture the gains while junior lawyers face a narrowing market and eroding wage premium. The distributional harm does not require mass unemployment — it only requires that the tasks AI automates are the ones that justified hiring large cohorts of graduates in the first place.

Berg (2026) calls this the institutional reclassification: returns that flowed as wages now flow as profit. It is the same logic as the power loom, the spreadsheet, and every previous general-purpose technology. The difference is that AI is restructuring knowledge work — the domain that previously offered white-collar workers protection from automation — and it is doing so while Australia's regulatory framework remains voluntary, fragmented, and structurally designed for a world without AI intermediaries.

Bromberg (2025) said Australia has barely begun the law reform exercise that AI demands. The ALRC President is right. The firms at the frontier are not waiting for the law to catch up. They are cutting 31% of their graduate intake and billing at partner rates for AI-generated work while the regulatory debate focuses on whether to write one AI Act or amend existing legislation.

The question this essay asks is not whether the billing hour will die. It is who determines where the value goes when it does — and whether Australia's institutions move fast enough to make that determination before the market makes it for them.

"Whether Artificial Intelligence is regulated and whatever form that regulation takes, AI is and will remain an agent of change that will transform the legal profession. And so it should."

— Justice Mordy Bromberg, ALRC President, Australian Law Forum, 14 August 2025
