01

The Central Argument

The economic question is not whether AI will change legal services. It is who captures the gains.

August 2025 · Canberra

The Largest Law Reform Exercise Australia Has Ever Faced

In August 2025, the President of the Australian Law Reform Commission walked to the podium at the inaugural Australian Law Forum and delivered a diagnosis that most of his audience was not ready to hear. AI regulation, said Justice Mordy Bromberg, is "the largest and most complex law reform exercise that currently confronts Australia." And the public debate, he continued, has barely recognised it is a law reform exercise at all (Bromberg 2025).

Bromberg's point was not that artificial intelligence will destroy the legal profession. He was careful to say the opposite — that forecasts of mass unemployment among lawyers are "likely misplaced," that AI cannot replicate the wisdom, the interpersonal trust, or the contextual judgment that makes a good lawyer valuable. What he was saying is more precise and more troubling: that the law was designed for a world without AI intermediaries, and that Australia is making regulatory decisions before it has done the foundational work of identifying what harms AI creates and whether current law addresses them.

This essay makes a more specific argument. The disruption AI is bringing to Australian legal services is not primarily a story about robots replacing lawyers. It is a story about who captures the productivity gains when artificial intelligence automates the cognitive tasks that junior lawyers have always performed — and who bears the cost when those tasks disappear.

"If we do not do that, the law will be left to chase a tail it will never ever catch."

— Justice Mordy Bromberg, ALRC President, Australian Law Forum, 14 August 2025

The distributional question is the economic question. Productivity gains from AI in legal services are real — a task that once consumed 4.5 hours of a junior lawyer's time now takes 30 minutes at Holding Redlich (CAIDE 2025). MinterEllison's Lantern AI processes 3,500 documents per hour, 58 times faster than manual review, saving $1.2–$1.5 million per major matter. These are not predictions. They are current facts. The question this essay addresses is where the value created by that speed goes — and what Australia must do before the answer is simply: upward.

2024–2025: The Quiet Revolution

While the public debates whether AI will replace lawyers, the profession is already reorganising around it.

2023–2025 · Australian Law Firms

Firm by Firm, the Work Moves Up the Chain

The adoption did not happen in a single announcement. It happened in a sequence of quiet decisions made by managing partners in conference rooms across Sydney and Melbourne, each one looking at a tool that could do in minutes what a graduate had always done in hours.

In August 2023, Allens became the first major Australian firm to deploy a firm-wide generative AI platform — "Airlie," built on Microsoft Azure. The announcement was carefully worded: AI would "automate routine tasks, support drafting efforts, and improve efficiency." No mention of headcount. No mention of graduate intake. Just efficiency (CAIDE 2025).

By February 2025, Gilbert + Tobin had launched Harvey AI across its 650-lawyer team. Ashurst had signed a global partnership with Harvey making it the first firm to give every lawyer and staff member access across every practice group. MinterEllison had deployed Lantern for discovery, processing 3,500 documents per hour. Holding Redlich had permanently adopted Lexis+ AI after a partner reported completing a research task in 30 minutes that had previously taken 4.5 hours. Clayton Utz was using Lexis+ AI to generate first drafts of client advice, internal emails, and court filings (CAIDE 2025).

Then came the signal that changed the conversation. In 2025, MinterEllison — one of Australia's top four law firms — announced it was reducing its graduate intake from 104 to 72 positions. A nearly 31% cut. Its chief people officer Rachel Banks stated directly that "responsible use of AI is improving efficiency in routine work graduates traditionally start on, while demand shifts towards complex matters" (Lawyers Weekly 2025).

This was not a prediction about the future. It was a statement about the present: the tasks that justified hiring 104 graduates no longer required 104 graduates. The work had not gone away. As one legal consultant put it: "In simple terms, the work hasn't gone away. It's moved up the chain" (Law Society Journal 2025).

The billable hours data confirmed it. Non-equity partners and junior to mid-level associates recorded their third consecutive productivity decline in the first half of the 2025/26 financial year. Senior associates and equity partners logged more hours. The profession's internal labour market was reorganising — with value flowing to the top, and volume being absorbed by machines.

The Adoption Gap

74% of large law firms (200+ employees) have formal AI policies in place. Only 25% of small firms do (Agile Market Intelligence 2026). This is not just a technology adoption story — it is a market concentration story. The firms with the resources to move first are building capability advantages that small practices and community legal centres cannot easily match.

Who has deployed what Allens: "Airlie" (Azure, 2023). Gilbert + Tobin: Harvey AI (650 lawyers, Feb 2025). Ashurst: Harvey (firm-wide, Jun 2024). MinterEllison: Lantern discovery AI + Copilot. Holding Redlich: Lexis+ AI permanently. Clayton Utz: Lexis+ AI for first drafts.
Critical note Despite MinterEllison's cuts, aggregate graduate hiring across Australian firms rose 6% in 2025. Disruption is compositional, not yet aggregate — large firms are restructuring while small firms have not yet moved.

The Economic Mechanism

Why this pattern was predictable — and what the task-based framework says about where it goes next.

Economic Theory

Displacement, Reinstatement, and the Jagged Frontier

The economic framework for understanding what is happening in Australian law firms was built long before ChatGPT existed. Acemoglu and Restrepo (2019) model technological change as operating through two opposing forces on labour markets. The displacement effect: capital substitutes for labour in tasks labour previously performed, reducing the labour share of value added. The reinstatement effect: new tasks are created in which labour has a comparative advantage, recovering the labour share. The net outcome depends on which force dominates, and on the size of the productivity gain from automation.

The task taxonomy that makes this operational comes from Autor, Levy and Murnane (2003): computers substitute most readily for routine cognitive tasks — information processing, pattern recognition, rule-following, document analysis. They complement non-routine cognitive tasks — complex judgment, novel problem-solving, persuasion, contextual reasoning. Legal services sit at a precise fault line between these categories. Document review is routine. Novel legal argument is not. Junior lawyers do mostly the former. Senior partners do mostly the latter.

Martin et al. (2024) tested this directly — comparing large language models against junior lawyers and legal process outsourcers on real contract review tasks, with senior lawyers setting the benchmark. The result was stark: advanced LLMs matched or exceeded junior lawyer accuracy, completed reviews in seconds rather than hours, and operated at a 99.97% cost reduction. The tasks most exposed to AI substitution are precisely the entry-level tasks through which junior lawyers build their professional experience and earn their early career wage premium.

Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond (2023) measured the productivity effect in a real deployment — a generative AI assistant introduced to 5,172 customer support agents. Average productivity rose 15%. But the distribution mattered: the largest gains went to the least experienced workers. AI was closing the gap between novices and experts by giving novices access to expert-level guidance. For legal services, this is a double-edged finding: it means AI genuinely helps junior lawyers perform better in the short run, while simultaneously undermining the wage premium that justified hiring them in large numbers.

One important caveat: Acemoglu (2024) argues in a peer-reviewed paper that the macroeconomic productivity gains from AI are likely far smaller than popular accounts suggest — estimating total factor productivity gains of less than 0.66% over the next decade, because only around 20% of labour tasks are currently exposed to profitable automation. This essay does not dispute the aggregate modesty of the macroeconomic impact. The argument here operates at the sector and task level: even if AI adds little to national GDP, it can substantially restructure who performs which tasks within the legal profession — and the distributional consequences of that restructuring are what matter for the 97,000 Australians who work in legal services.

Furthermore, Brynjolfsson et al. (2023) identify a "jagged frontier" — a boundary inside which AI dramatically improves performance, and outside which it actively harms it. Workers who over-relied on AI for tasks requiring genuine contextual judgment performed 19% worse than those working without AI. This is the verification risk that Australian law societies are already warning about (ACT Law Society 2024): junior lawyers learning to generate answers quickly without developing the independent judgment to evaluate them.

Acemoglu's sceptical case Acemoglu (2024) predicts TFP gains from AI of only 0.53–0.66% over 10 years. The disruption at sector and task level can be significant even if the macroeconomic aggregate is modest.
The reinstatement question Acemoglu & Restrepo (2019) show new tasks historically absorb displaced workers. In legal services, AI governance law, data privacy specialisms, and AI output supervision are candidate new tasks — but whether they absorb as many as they replace remains genuinely uncertain.

Who Captures the Gains?

The productivity is real. The question is whether it flows to partners, clients, or workers.

The Distributional Question

Wages to Profit: The Institutional Reclassification

The billing data answers the distributional question more clearly than the employment data. Only 6% of Australian law firms charge clients less for AI-assisted work. A further 34% charge more. The remaining 58% have not changed their rates at all (Law Society Journal 2025). The productivity gains created by AI tools — tools that compress hours of junior work into minutes — are being retained by firms, not passed to clients.

This is the institutional reclassification that Berg (2026) identifies in the broader economy: returns that previously flowed as wages to workers now flow as profit to capital owners. Before AI, a law firm hired a junior associate, paid wages, and the value of the associate's document review was split between the associate (wage) and the firm (profit margin). After AI, the firm subscribes to Harvey, pays API fees, and the value of the same document review flows entirely to the firm — and to Anthropic or OpenAI as the vendor of the underlying model.

The junior associate is not necessarily fired. But the number of such positions required shrinks. The positions that remain increasingly involve supervising AI output rather than producing legal work independently. And the experience that supervision provides is different — arguably shallower — than the experience of producing document review from scratch. The LexisNexis Mentorship Gap Report (2026) found that 72% of law firm professionals identified deep legal reasoning and argumentation as the largest skills gap among junior lawyers, and 69% pointed to weak verification skills. The apprenticeship model through which the profession has always trained its future partners is being disrupted by the very productivity tools that make firms more profitable in the short run.

"Work that previously took a junior four hours is now being completed in under one hour via the latest AI. In simple terms, the work hasn't gone away. It's moved up the chain."

— Nandan Subramaniam, principal at Zed Law, quoted in Law Society Journal (2025)

There is a competing argument. The International Bar Association (2024) notes that 52% of in-house legal teams expect bills to decrease as AI adoption spreads — suggesting that competitive pressure will eventually force firms to pass gains to clients. Goldman Sachs estimates that 44% of legal work tasks could be automated. If that automation eventually reaches pricing, clients — particularly corporations with legal departments sophisticated enough to demand it — may capture much of the value. In that scenario, the distributional harm falls not on clients but on the legal profession itself, and particularly on the junior lawyers whose wage premium erodes as their tasks become automatable.

Either way, the concentration dynamic is real. The 74% versus 25% AI policy gap between large and small firms is already widening into a capability gap. If large commercial firms can deploy Harvey AI, train it on their own matter data, and offer faster and cheaper service than regional or boutique practices, the result is market concentration — fewer competitive suppliers, reduced options for clients who cannot afford the firms at the frontier, and a legal profession increasingly stratified between those who work with AI and those who are priced out of it.

Three Scenarios to 2030

The trajectory is not predetermined. It depends on adoption pace, reinstatement, and institutional response.

2025–2030

Baseline, Upside, and Downside

The following scenarios share the same starting conditions — the facts established above. They diverge based on three key variables: the pace of AI adoption by small firms, the emergence of new legal tasks through the reinstatement effect, and the speed of Australia's institutional response.

Baseline (2025–2030): Concentrated Gains, Narrowing Pipeline

Key assumption: Adoption continues at current pace. Large firms widen their advantage. Voluntary regulation persists. New AI governance roles emerge but in smaller numbers than displaced positions.

  • Graduate intakes at top-tier firms continue to shrink 5–10% annually through 2028 as AI handles document review and research
  • Mid-tier and small firms adopt AI more slowly due to cost constraints — the 74% vs 25% policy gap becomes a capability gap
  • Equity partner and senior associate billing rates rise as relative scarcity of judgment-intensive work increases their value
  • New roles emerge — AI output reviewers, legal data scientists — but in smaller numbers than junior positions lost
  • Australia issues updated voluntary guidance but no national AI statute — fragmented state-by-state framework persists
  • The access-to-justice gap widens as small firms fall behind in capability; community legal centres remain underresourced
Policy trigger: A high-profile AI legal error — fabricated precedent used in a Federal Court submission, or a privilege waiver causing regulatory harm to a corporate client — forces a reckoning and accelerates the ALRC's law reform exercise.

Upside (2025–2030): Productivity Shared, Access Expanded

Key assumption: Competition forces firms to pass AI productivity gains to clients. New legal tasks — AI governance law, algorithmic accountability, access-to-justice tools — absorb displaced workers. Institutions adapt faster than the baseline assumes.

  • Competitive pressure from AI-empowered clients (Susskind 2024) forces firms to reduce hourly rates — the 52% of in-house teams expecting lower bills get them
  • AI-powered access-to-justice tools (amica.gov.au, SmartAssist, NALA) expand legal help to underserved Australians, reducing the access gap
  • Law schools redesign curricula around AI-complementary skills — critical reasoning, client management, AI output verification — maintaining the quality of junior lawyers entering the profession
  • New legal specialisms in AI governance, data privacy, and algorithmic accountability create genuine reinstatement of professional employment
  • Australia adopts a national AI liability framework that creates clear accountability without creating compliance costs that entrench BigLaw
What has to be true: Competition is sufficient to force price reductions before market concentration entrenches large-firm dominance. The reinstatement effect operates strongly enough to offset displacement at the junior level. Government acts before the access-to-justice gap becomes irreversible.

Downside (2025–2030): Concentration, Exclusion, Regulatory Failure

Key assumption: Large firms capture gains without passing them on. Regulatory vacuum persists. AI errors cause systemic client harm. The profession stratifies into two tiers.

  • The 74% vs 25% AI adoption gap becomes permanent — large firms pull decisively ahead, small firms lose clients to AI-enabled commercial practices
  • Graduate unemployment in law rises as aggregate intake falls and new roles do not materialise at scale
  • AI legal errors — hallucinated citations in court submissions, inadvertent privilege waivers — generate client harm without clear liability pathways
  • The billing hour survives nominally but loses meaning — firms bill at partner rates for AI-generated work that was never reviewed with genuine care
  • ASIC's 2026 accountability agenda creates compliance costs that disproportionately burden small practices without adequate guidance
  • Australia develops a two-tier legal profession: frontier AI-equipped commercial firms serving corporate clients, and under-resourced smaller practices serving everyone else
What triggers this: Regulatory inaction past 2027. A major AI legal scandal that erodes public trust without producing legislative response. Failure to develop national standards for AI competence in legal practice before market concentration becomes entrenched.

The difference between these scenarios is not, primarily, about the technology. AI will develop along roughly similar lines in all three. The difference is institutional: how quickly Australia identifies the gaps in its legal framework, whether regulatory design is calibrated to protect both innovation and equity, and whether the profession itself builds the training structures needed to maintain the quality of lawyers who will practise in an AI-augmented world.

What Australia Must Do

Three concrete gaps and the regulatory instruments to close them — without entrenching the firms that are already winning.

Policy Analysis

Liability Over Licensing — and Three Gaps That Cannot Wait

Australia's current position is coherent but insufficient. The December 2025 National AI Plan confirmed that existing laws — the Corporations Act, the Privacy Act, professional conduct rules — will govern AI for now, supplemented by voluntary guidance (Australian Government 2025). ASIC declared 2026 its "Year of Accountability," signalling that directors who fail to oversee AI risks face civil penalties and criminal referrals (Brief Online 2026). State law societies have issued guidance on confidentiality and hallucination risks (ACT Law Society 2024).

But Bromberg (2025) is correct that the debate has fixated on form — one AI Act versus amending existing laws — rather than substance. The foundational exercise of identifying what harms AI creates in legal services, and whether current law addresses them, has not been done. Until it is, regulation will lag the technology it is supposed to govern.

Three gaps are concrete enough to act on now.

Gap 1: Liability for AI legal errors. When an AI-drafted contract causes client harm, existing law places liability on the supervising lawyer. But this assumes lawyers can meaningfully supervise AI output — a questionable assumption when AI systems hallucinate with apparent confidence. The Law Council's guidance that "the duty of competence extends to supervising AI output as it does to supervising a junior solicitor" is correct in principle but creates an accountability standard that is unenforceable without clearer definitions of what adequate supervision requires (ACT Law Society 2024). Kretschmer et al. (2023) argue that making developers and deployers financially liable for AI-caused harms — rather than regulating inputs through pre-market approval — creates better incentives for safety without specifying in advance exactly what "safe" AI looks like.

Gap 2: Legal professional privilege. When client data is processed through third-party AI platforms, privilege may be inadvertently waived because the data has been disclosed to a non-lawyer third party. The Heppner case (US, 2026) established this risk in federal court. Queensland's Supreme Court has already warned that "any information input into public chatbots should be seen as published." Australian courts have not yet ruled definitively, but the risk is real and currently unmanaged by any binding national standard (Hamilton Locke 2025).

Gap 3: Market concentration and access to justice. The 74% versus 25% AI policy gap between large and small firms is a market structure problem. Kretschmer et al. (2023) warn that EU-style risk-based AI regulation tends to entrench incumbents by imposing compliance costs that large firms absorb easily and small firms cannot. Australia must design any regulatory response with this explicitly in mind — targeted interventions that protect small practices and community legal centres rather than creating bureaucratic hurdles that advantage the firms already at the frontier.

Immediate (2026)

  • Law Council to issue binding national AI competence standards — not voluntary guidance
  • Privacy Act amendments to explicitly address privilege in AI-processed client data
  • ASIC to issue sector-specific AI governance guidance for legal practices

Short Term (2027–2028)

  • ALRC to complete full statutory review identifying liability gaps created by AI in regulated professions
  • Federal Court to finalise its AI practice note, creating a national standard for AI-generated submissions
  • Subsidised AI tool access for community legal centres and small firms

Medium Term (2029–2030)

  • National liability framework for AI in high-stakes professional services — allocating responsibility between developers, deployers, and practitioners
  • Law school accreditation updated to require AI competence and verification training
  • Mandatory client disclosure when AI systems are used in client-facing legal work

What to Avoid

  • EU-style ex-ante conformity assessment — compliance costs would entrench BigLaw (Kretschmer et al. 2023)
  • Technology-specific legislation that becomes obsolete before it is enforced
  • Treating AI as equivalent to existing legal software — Bromberg is right that it is "a different species of technology"

The preferred instrument is liability, not licensing. A well-designed liability framework signals to AI developers and law firm deployers that if an AI-generated document causes client harm, someone bears the cost. That creates incentives to invest in accuracy and supervision without requiring regulators to predict in advance exactly what form those investments should take (Kretschmer et al. 2023). In a fast-moving technology, this is more robust than prescriptive rules drafted before the technology stabilises.

ASIC's position Section 180 of the Corporations Act requires directors to exercise independent judgment on material risks. "I did not know what the algorithm did" provides no legal defence. ASIC is targeting directors — not just firms (Brief Online 2026).
Privacy Act (Dec 2026) New amendments require organisations using substantially automated decision-making that significantly affects individuals to disclose this in their privacy policy. Most Australian law firms are not yet ready for this obligation.

Conclusion

The billing hour is not dead. But the foundations beneath it are being hollowed out.

2025–2030 · The Stakes

The Gains Are Real. The Question Is Who Keeps Them.

The disruption AI is bringing to Australian legal services did not begin with a manifesto or a press release. It began in conference rooms where managing partners looked at tools that could do in 30 minutes what a junior had always done in 4.5 hours, and decided that efficiency was the imperative. It has proceeded through a series of firm-level decisions — Harvey at Gilbert + Tobin, Lantern at MinterEllison, Lexis+ AI at Holding Redlich — each individually sensible, collectively transformative.

The economic mechanism is well understood. Autor, Levy and Murnane (2003) predicted that computers would substitute for routine cognitive tasks. Acemoglu and Restrepo (2019) showed that the distributional outcome depended on whether new tasks were created to absorb displaced workers. Brynjolfsson et al. (2023) showed that generative AI raises productivity, with the largest gains for the least experienced workers — precisely the junior associates whose tasks AI is automating, and whose career pipeline is narrowing as a result.

Acemoglu (2024) urges caution about the scale of the macroeconomic disruption — and he may well be right that the aggregate productivity impact is modest. But the question this essay addresses is not aggregate GDP. It is the distributional question: who captures the productivity gains, who bears the adjustment costs, and what Australian institutions must do to shape a better answer than the market will produce on its own.

Right now, the answer the market is producing is: equity partners and AI vendors capture the gains. Junior lawyers and small firms bear the cost. Community legal centres remain underresourced. And Australia's regulatory framework — voluntary guidance from state law societies, no national AI statute, unresolved liability for AI legal errors, no clarity on privilege — is not designed to change that answer.

Bromberg (2025) said that AI is a "different species of technology" — not something we build, but something we train, something that directs itself. That autonomy creates harms the law was not designed to address. The ALRC President is right that Australia has not yet recognised the scale of the law reform exercise this demands. The firms at the frontier are not waiting for the law to catch up. They are hiring 58-times-faster document review systems and cutting 31% of their graduate intake while the regulatory debate focuses on whether to write one AI Act or amend existing legislation.

The billing hour is not dead. But the junior lawyers who would have worked those hours are fewer than they used to be, their tasks have moved up the chain, and the value they created is being booked as profit rather than wages. That is not a prediction about 2030. It is a description of 2025.

The question is what Australia does about it before the window to shape the outcome closes.

"Whether Artificial Intelligence is regulated and whatever form that regulation takes, AI is and will remain an agent of change that will transform the legal profession. And so it should."

— Justice Mordy Bromberg, ALRC President, 14 August 2025
