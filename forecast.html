Section 01 —

The Central Argument

Not whether AI will change legal services, but who captures the gains.

The Distributional Question

A Different Species of Technology Demands a Different Kind of Analysis

In August 2025, the President of the Australian Law Reform Commission told the Australian Law Forum that AI regulation is "the largest and most complex law reform exercise that currently confronts Australia", and that the public debate had barely recognised it was a law reform exercise at all (Bromberg 2025). This official ALRC intervention frames the legal-policy problem: AI is not just another product liability issue, but a technology that is trained, adaptive, and increasingly capable of directing itself.

This essay makes a narrower economic claim. Using the task-based framework of Acemoglu and Restrepo (2019), Australian occupation data on legal work, LLM exposure evidence, and Berg's capital-labour hybrid framing, the disruption AI brings to Australian legal services is a story about who captures the productivity gains when AI automates document-heavy tasks previously performed by junior lawyers. Current Australian firm-level evidence suggests those gains are already measurable; the theoretical question is where the value flows.

Central Forecast

By 2027–2030, AI will automate a substantial share of junior associate task hours at large Australian commercial law firms. Unless reinstatement through new AI-supervision, governance, and client-facing tasks is strong enough to offset displacement, productivity gains will concentrate among equity partners and AI vendors. The junior career pipeline will narrow, and Australia's fragmented voluntary regulatory framework will struggle to shape how these gains are distributed without structural reform.

Section 02 —

2023–2026: The Quiet Revolution

Firm by firm, the work moves up the chain.

Australian Law Firms · 2023–2026

While the Debate Continues, the Profession Reorganises

The adoption curve moved in stages. First came controlled internal experimentation: Allens launched its GenAI tool Airlie in August 2023. Then AI moved into production tasks: Holding Redlich permanently adopted Lexis+ AI after a lawyer replicated a 4.5-hour research task in 30 minutes. By 2025, adoption had scaled across whole firms and high-volume workflows: Gilbert + Tobin launched Harvey AI across 650 lawyers, while MinterEllison's Lantern processed 3,500 discovery documents per hour, 58 times faster than manual review, saving $1.2–$1.5 million per matter (CAIDE 2025).

Then came an early hiring signal: MinterEllison became the first major Australian law firm to publicly link a graduate intake reduction to AI, cutting its 2025–26 cohort by almost one-third to 72, partly because AI was automating routine lower-level work (Tadros 2026). The hours data points in the same direction: demand remains solid, but non-equity partners and junior/mid-level associates are logging fewer hours while senior associates and equity partners are logging more. This does not prove causation, but it is consistent with GenAI compressing research, drafting and document-review work and shifting value toward legal judgment, supervision and client accountability (Thomson Reuters 2026; McKeith 2026).

Section 03 —

The Economic Mechanism

Why this pattern was predictable, and where the theory says it goes next.

Economic Theory Applied to Australian Law

Displacement, Reinstatement, and the Diffusion Lag

Acemoglu and Restrepo (2019) frame automation as a contest between displacement, where capital takes over existing labour tasks, and reinstatement, where new tasks restore demand for labour. The distributional outcome depends on which force dominates. For legal services, the exposure claim can be built task-by-task. ABS OSCA shows that Australian solicitor, law clerk, legal assistant and paralegal roles are heavily document- and research-based (ABS 2024a; ABS 2024b).

Those tasks match the exposure pattern Eloundou et al. (2024) identify: language-heavy, information-processing work is exposed to LLMs because software can reduce the time required to produce a usable output without requiring physical dexterity or open-ended judgment. The document-heavy, codifiable work of junior lawyers fits exactly that description — the routine tasks Autor, Levy and Murnane (2003) showed are most vulnerable to automation because they can be reduced to rules a machine can follow. Robb et al. (2025) confirm the implication for Australian legal practice specifically, warning that GenAI may reduce junior research, review and drafting work and create a "5-year problem" for junior skill formation. The hours data already shows the mechanism in motion: junior billable hours are falling while senior hours rise (Thomson Reuters 2026; McKeith 2026). The MinterEllison intake cut and the hours shift are early evidence that displacement is outpacing reinstatement — junior task-hours are shrinking before equivalent new tasks have appeared to replace them.

Berg (2026) places this in the broader GPT framework: like electricity and computing before it, AI's full impact requires complementary organisational change, not just tool adoption. Australian firms are currently in the bolt-on phase, deploying AI onto existing workflows rather than redesigning around what AI can actually do. When that redesign occurs, displacement may intensify.

Section 04 —

Who Gains, Who Bears the Risk?

The productivity is real. The distribution is the problem.

The Distributional Mechanism

How Do Productivity Gains Become Market Advantage?

Berg's capital-labour hybrid framework treats AI as capital in institutional form but labour-like in production: it is owned and monetised as capital while supplying cognitive services previously performed by workers (Berg 2026). Applied to legal services, this suggests that work once billed through junior labour may be partly converted into firm profit and AI vendor revenue, rather than automatically flowing to workers or clients. Current pricing evidence provides partial support for that theory: only 6% of Australian law firms lower prices for AI-assisted work, while 34% charge more and 58% have not changed rates (McKeith 2026). Acemoglu (2024) provides the macroeconomic version of the same point: AI may not reduce labour-income inequality and is predicted to widen the gap between capital and labour income when returns from automation accrue to capital owners rather than workers, especially if the reinstatement effect is too weak, meaning new worker tasks are not created fast enough.

The governance risk also has a distributional effect. The Federal Court does not ban AI, but distinguishes public/open AI tools from closed or controlled systems: public tools create higher confidentiality and privilege risks, while closed systems may reduce those risks only if firms preserve confidentiality rigorously (Federal Court of Australia 2026). This makes AI governance more than a privacy issue; it is also a market-competitiveness problem. Smaller firms and their clients may face higher hidden costs because safe AI use requires secure systems, verification processes, staff training and risk controls. Larger firms are better placed to absorb those fixed costs and invest in dedicated internal or enterprise AI systems, allowing them to capture productivity gains with lower governance risk (Federal Court of Australia 2026; Legal Services Research Centre 2026; CAIDE 2025). The OECD (2025) measures this structural gap at scale: 52% of large firms now use AI compared with 17.4% of small firms — a difference that reflects not just early adoption but compounding advantage in resources, infrastructure, and risk capacity.

"Work that previously took a junior four hours is now being completed in under one hour via the latest AI. The work hasn't gone away. It's moved up the chain."

— Nandan Subramaniam, Zed Law principal, Law Society Journal (2026)

Section 05 —

Three Scenarios to 2030

The trajectory is not predetermined. Institutional response is the critical variable.

Scenario Context

Whether those gains concentrate or broadly diffuse depends on which of three plausible trajectories Australian legal services take over the next five years. The distributional model in Section 04 shows the mechanism is real; these scenarios test what determines the outcome — specifically, how quickly reinstatement creates new tasks, whether regulatory action arrives before market concentration locks in, and whether competitive pressure forces firms to share gains with clients and workers.

2025–2030 · Forecast

Baseline, Upside, Downside — Compared Across Four Economic Mechanisms

Baseline: Concentrated Gains, Narrowing Pipeline (Most Likely)

Scenario logic: AI adoption continues unevenly across firm size: 52% of large firms now use AI compared with 17.4% of small firms (OECD 2025). Large Australian commercial firms use this advantage to raise productivity while retaining gains; only 6% pass savings to clients (McKeith 2026). New AI-governance roles are emerging but in smaller numbers than displaced junior positions. This mirrors how previous general-purpose technologies behaved, where reinstatement through new tasks was slow pending training reform and institutional adjustment (Acemoglu and Restrepo 2019). National statutory governance of AI in legal services remains absent, compounding the adoption gap.

Trigger: A high-profile AI legal error (fabricated precedent in a Federal Court submission, or an inadvertent privilege waiver causing ASIC enforcement) forces binding national standards.

Upside: Productivity Shared, Access Expanded

Scenario logic: AI tools become accessible beyond large firms, genuine competition emerges, and productivity gains transfer to consumers through lower fees. New AI occupations within legal services emerge at sufficient scale to offset displacement (Acemoglu and Restrepo 2019).

What must be true: Government must subsidise or regulate access before the adoption gap (OECD 2025) becomes irreversible. Law school training must equip new graduates to work alongside AI, the reform Robb et al. (2025) argue is necessary but not yet occurring.

Downside: Concentration, Exclusion, Regulatory Failure

Scenario logic: Large firms capture AI gains without competitive or regulatory pressure to share them; 34% already charge more for AI-assisted work (McKeith 2026). Regulatory inaction past 2027 allows market concentration to entrench before standards are set. Simshaw (2022) identifies this as the core access-to-justice risk: when only well-resourced firms can effectively deploy legal AI, disadvantaged groups are relegated to inferior tools or no assistance. Kretschmer et al. (2023) warn that compliance-heavy regulation enacted late worsens concentration by creating barriers only large firms can absorb.

  • AI-generated work is billed at premium rates without genuine supervision.
  • Smaller practices face higher compliance burdens and weaker tool access.
  • A two-tier profession emerges between AI-equipped commercial firms and under-resourced practices.
Trigger: Regulatory inaction past 2027. Major AI legal scandal without legislative response. Market concentration entrenched before standards are set.

Section 06 —

What Australia Must Do

Three concrete gaps, and instruments to close them without entrenching BigLaw.

Policy Recommendations

Liability Over Licensing — Before the Window Closes

The three scenarios above diverge on a single question: whether Australia closes three governance gaps before market concentration becomes irreversible. Australia's current position, comprising existing laws supplemented by voluntary state guidance (Australian Government 2025), ASIC's "Year of Accountability" (Brief Online 2026), and state court practice notes (VLSB+C, Law Society of NSW and LPBWA 2024), is coherent but structurally inadequate. Bromberg (2025) identifies the core problem: Australia is debating the form of AI regulation (one Act versus amending existing laws) without first identifying the harms AI creates and whether current law addresses them. Three gaps demand immediate attention.

Gap 1: Liability for AI legal errors. When AI-drafted work causes client harm, liability falls on the supervising lawyer, but no binding standard defines what adequate supervision requires (VLSB+C, Law Society of NSW and LPBWA 2024). Kretschmer et al. (2023) propose differentiating endogenous harm (developers or deployers could have foreseen and prevented the failure, so strict liability applies) from exogenous harm (genuinely unforeseeable, attracting limited liability). This allocates incentives correctly without over-regulating innovation and is more robust than prescriptive ex-ante licensing that becomes obsolete before it can be enforced.

Gap 2: Legal professional privilege. The Federal Court's 2026 practice note warns that information entered into generally accessible GenAI tools may become available to others and that privileged, confidential, suppressed or private information must not be entered into GenAI where doing so breaches legal obligations (Federal Court of Australia 2026). The joint NSW, Victorian and WA regulators statement is similarly explicit: practitioners cannot safely input confidential, sensitive or privileged client information into public AI tools (VLSB+C, Law Society of NSW and LPBWA 2024). Yet Australia still relies on overlapping court and professional guidance rather than a single national standard for legal AI governance.

Gap 3: Market concentration. Kretschmer et al. (2023) warn that EU-style risk-based regulation tends to entrench incumbents by imposing compliance costs large firms absorb and small firms cannot. Australia must calibrate any regulatory response so it protects small practices and community legal centres rather than creating barriers that accelerate BigLaw dominance.

Immediate 2026 — Law Council + ASIC

  • Binding national AI supervision standards, not voluntary guidance
  • ASIC sector-specific AI governance guidance for legal practices
  • KPI: 90% of top-50 firms with documented AI supervision protocols by Dec 2026

Short Term 2027–28 — ALRC + Federal Court

  • ALRC statutory review: identify liability gaps; enact developer/deployer liability allocation (Kretschmer et al. 2023)
  • Federal Court finalises AI practice note, establishing a national standard for AI-generated submissions
  • KPI: Privilege waiver risk addressed in binding guidelines across all federal jurisdictions

Medium Term 2029–30 — Parliament + Law Schools

  • National AI liability framework for professional services, enacted rather than voluntary
  • Law school accreditation updated to require AI competence and verification training
  • Subsidised AI access program for community legal centres and firms under 10 staff

What to Avoid

  • EU-style ex-ante conformity assessment, which entrenches BigLaw through compliance costs (Kretschmer et al. 2023)
  • Technology-specific legislation that becomes obsolete before enforced
  • Regulatory inaction past 2027: the window to shape distribution is closing

Section 07 —

Conclusion

The gains are real. The question is who keeps them.

2025–2030 · The Stakes

Before the Market Makes the Decision for Us

The reorganisation documented in this essay is already happening: graduate intakes cut, junior hours falling, productivity gains retained by firms. The distributional question — who captures the gains when AI automates junior tasks — is not a forecast. It is the current state of Australian legal services, with the regulatory framework still catching up.

The critical variable is not AI capability but institutional speed. Acemoglu (2024) shows the capital–labour income gap widens when reinstatement is too slow; Kretschmer et al. (2023) show that late regulation entrenches incumbents. Australia has a narrow window — Bromberg (2025) puts it plainly — before the market resolves the distributional question entirely in favour of those already winning. One important uncertainty remains open: reinstatement is economy-wide, and displaced junior lawyers may find roles in AI governance or adjacent knowledge work outside legal services. Whether that offset is sufficient is genuinely unknown.

Bromberg (2025) warned that without foundational law reform, "the law will be left to chase a tail it will never ever catch." The tail is moving fast.

"Whether Artificial Intelligence is regulated and whatever form that regulation takes, AI is and will remain an agent of change that will transform the legal profession. And so it should."

— Justice Mordy Bromberg, ALRC President, Australian Law Forum, 14 August 2025
